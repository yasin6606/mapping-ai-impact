Comparing categories · Electricity context for AI

Same demand.
Different electricity stories.

Six European countries · National generation · 2025

AI infrastructure needs electricity. The mix behind that electricity differs by country. These five views compare the national generation context, helping explain why location matters when discussing environmental impact.

What these data describe

All national electricity generation, including uses beyond data centres. They do not identify the electricity purchased by an individual data centre or isolate AI’s share.

01 / Bar chart

Same electricity, different carbon context

How does the lifecycle carbon intensity of national generation compare?

Germany: 329.65 gCO₂e/kWh; Sweden: 35.26. The difference is about ninefold within this sample.

Why this chart. Bar length supports accurate ranking. A zero baseline preserves the size of the differences; labels give the values.

Read with care. Generation-weighted annual national averages include lifecycle emissions. They are not marginal, hourly or consumption-based grid factors, and do not measure emissions from particular AI systems.

Source: Ember, via Our World in Data · 2025 · retrieved 17 Nov 2026.

02 / Grouped bar chart

Wind and solar play different roles

Which of these two technologies contributes more to each country’s electricity?

  • Wind
  • Solar

Wind contributes more than solar in France, Germany, the Netherlands and Sweden. Solar leads in Italy and Spain.

Why this chart. Pairs of bars share one scale and one zero baseline, making within-country and between-country comparisons direct.

Read with care. Shares use each country’s total generation as the denominator. Similar percentages can represent very different TWh, and these two technologies are only part of the mix.

Source: Ember, via Our World in Data · 2025 · retrieved 17 Nov 2026.

04 / 100% stacked bar chart

Low-carbon does not mean the same mix

How do fossil, renewable and nuclear sources divide national generation?

  • Fossil
  • Renewables
  • Nuclear

France’s large nuclear share and Sweden’s large renewable share are two different routes to predominantly low-carbon electricity.

Why this chart. Equal-length bars make composition comparable, independent of national generation volume. Colours and ordering remain consistent across charts.

Read with care. Internal segments have different starting points, so their lengths are harder to compare. This chart hides absolute TWh. Renewable generation includes bioenergy; low-carbon does not mean zero lifecycle emissions.

Why this chart. A familiar 10 × 10 grid communicates part-to-whole proportions without requiring readers to estimate angles or areas.

Read with care. Whole squares round the data. The largest-remainder method allocates exactly 100 squares; the adjacent percentages retain two decimals. All squares describe generation, not AI’s electricity share.

Sources & reproducibility

What the comparison can support

These countries are a purposive sample: Italy connects the project to its university setting; France, Germany, the Netherlands, Spain and Sweden provide contrasting electricity systems. They are not a ranking of data-centre markets or a representative sample of Europe.

Source. Ember’s Yearly Electricity Data Europe (2026), processed and distributed by Our World in Data. All charts use the same 2025 rows, downloaded on 17 November 2026. Data are a fixed snapshot; later revisions will not silently change this page.

Definitions. Fossil = coal, oil and gas. Renewables include hydro, wind, solar, bioenergy and other renewables. Low-carbon = renewables + nuclear. Shares refer to electricity generated within national borders, not electricity consumed after imports and exports. Carbon intensity includes lifecycle greenhouse gases, measured in grams of CO₂ equivalents per kWh generated.

Preparation. Select six countries and 2025, require every displayed indicator to be present, retain numeric source precision, and check fossil + renewables + nuclear equals 100% within 0.02 percentage points. No missing values were replaced with zero. Stacked geometry is normalised by each row’s sum to remove tiny source-rounding residuals; displayed values retain the source shares. Waffle counts use the largest-remainder method.

Limits. Annual averages hide hourly variation, imports, contractual procurement and marginal generation. This comparison cannot establish the footprint of a particular data centre, model or prompt. Turning intensity into emissions requires a clearly defined electricity-demand scenario, compatible system boundaries and a consistent time period.
