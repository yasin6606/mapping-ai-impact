The Impact of AI on the Environment and Energy Consumption

Group Insights · Yasin · Bahar · Pantea · Ferdaws

Every prompt, every training run, every data centre leaves a physical trace — in electricity, water and carbon. This site maps that footprint with open data so the invisible costs become visible.

1. Project Description

This website is the course deliverable of Group Insights for the University of Genova project Mapping the Impact of AI through Data. We focus on one urgent thread: how artificial intelligence reshapes energy systems and environmental pressure.

Training frontier models and running inference at global scale already draws tens of gigawatts. Data centres built for AI used roughly 155 TWh in 2025 — about one third of all data-centre electricity — and that share is rising fast. Cooling those facilities also consumes vast amounts of water. Our goal is to turn dense reports from the IEA, Stanford HAI, Epoch AI and operator disclosures into clear visual stories.

Data at hand

  • IEA – Key Questions on Energy and AI — global electricity demand of data centres and AI-focused facilities
  • Stanford HAI AI Index Report 2026 — power capacity, training emissions, water estimates
  • Epoch AI – Energy research — model training power and capacity trends
  • Our World in Data – Artificial Intelligence — long-run context for compute and infrastructure
  • Operator environmental reports (Google, Microsoft, Meta, AWS) — site-level water and electricity disclosures
485 TWh All data centres, 2025 (IEA)
+50% AI data-centre growth in 2025
29.6 GW AI data-centre power capacity
~950 TWh Projected data centres by 2030

Why this matters

  • AI-focused facilities already use about one third of global data-centre electricity and are the fastest-growing slice.
  • Training a single frontier model can emit tens of thousands of tonnes of CO₂ equivalent.
  • Inference water use for large models may exceed the annual drinking needs of millions of people.
  • Grid strain and new fossil generation risk undoing years of power-sector emissions progress in some regions.

3. Method & Principles

  1. Open data only — every figure is tied to a public report or dataset so numbers can be verified.
  2. SVG + CSS selectors — charts are pure SVG, styled exclusively through the external stylesheet (no inline styles).
  3. Accessible structure — semantic HTML5, clear heading hierarchy and keyboard-friendly navigation.
  4. University identity — Roboto Slab for titles, Fira Sans for body text, and a palette that still nods to UniGe while expressing the environmental theme.
